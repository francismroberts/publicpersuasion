The Five Weeks

🧭

Speech Analysis Framework

This isn't new material — it's the term's own vocabulary, reassembled into an order you can actually run a speech through. Use it on any assigned speech from Weeks 2–3 (MLK, Oprah, the Georgia Tech student, Reagan's Challenger address, Steve Jobs at Stanford, Mario Cuomo, Emma González, Jesse Jackson), or on your own draft of the Persuasive Policy Speech.

The six lenses run in the same order the course itself does: conviction comes before meaning, meaning before credibility, credibility before color, and logic/emotion before delivery — because each one is the professor's stated foundation for the one after it. You don't have to run all six every time. Drafting a script is mostly Lenses 1–5; a final rehearsal pass before recording is really just Lens 6.

Worth knowing None of these lenses are meant to be graded checkboxes. They're diagnostic — a way to notice what a speech is actually doing, and, on your own draft, what it isn't doing yet.

1. Conviction & Connection

Week 2's opening question: think of the best public speaker you've ever seen — what do you actually remember about them? The professor's answer, from years of asking that question, comes down to two things. Conviction: does the speaker actually believe this, or does it read as performed? Connection: are they reaching the actual people in front of them, through story and relatability, or just talking at an abstraction called "the audience"? You can't skip to connection without conviction first — the lecture's own line is that you can't give what you don't have.

Persuasion itself decomposes into three parts: a problem, a solution, and a speaker who actually believes in the fix. And conviction doesn't require fame or a big stage — the same lecture pairs MLK's "I Have a Dream" with Oprah's Golden Globes speech and a Georgia Tech student nobody in the room had heard of, on purpose.

Ask yourself
  • What problem is the speaker naming, and what's their proposed fix?
  • Do you believe the speaker believes this, or does it read as performed?
  • Who, specifically, is in the room — not "the audience" in the abstract, but the actual people this was written for?
  • Where does the speech show real connection (a story, something relatable) versus where it's just talking at people?
  • Own draft: what do you actually believe here? Would it survive your own private-writing test?

2. Moment & Meaning

Week 3 opens with Reagan's Challenger address: a speech happens at one shared moment in time, which is exactly what separates it from a memo. Three steps precede any drafting — it's a tradition, not a task; if you can't see the room, you can't write for it (Peggy Noonan pictured a specific family before writing Reagan's words); and only then, choose your meaning, since a speech doesn't have to live in a silo of "informative" or "persuasive."

Reagan's address is the sharpest version of this test: written and delivered within about twelve hours of the disaster, for five audiences at once — schoolchildren, the public, the astronauts' families, NASA, and the Soviets — under one unifying message.

Ask yourself
  • What specific moment, occasion, or event is this speech responding to?
  • Can you see the room the speaker was picturing? Can you picture your own?
  • Out of everything that could be said at this moment, what meaning did the speaker choose to make — and what did they leave out?
  • Is there more than one audience listening at once, the way Challenger had five? Does the speech serve all of them, or only the loudest one?

3. Credibility

Credibility splits into two independent poles — competence/authority and likeability/warmth — and losing either one is fast and often fatal. Rick Perry lost the 2012 Republican primary in one moment when he couldn't name a third federal agency (a competence failure); BP's Tony Hayward lost his job over "I'd like my life back" (a warmth failure). Most audiences are processing peripherally, not centrally, so credibility gets judged almost instantly — often before anyone consciously evaluates the argument.

Two concrete, learnable habits build both poles at once. Be organized — a roadmap or preview ("today I want to tell you three stories," Jobs's own line), clear transitions, and a closing review. Be a person — shorter words, shorter sentences, dependent clauses split into their own sentences, jargon dropped, key points flagged with the conversational "here's why it matters" colon trick, the draft read aloud.

Ask yourself
  • Is there a roadmap or preview near the start — some version of "today I want to tell you X things"?
  • Are there clear transitions between sections, or do you lose the thread moving from one part to the next?
  • Does it close with a review, or just stop?
  • Read a paragraph out loud — does it sound like a person talking, or like something written to be read silently?
  • Where is competence strongest? Where is warmth strongest? Is either pole missing entirely?

4. Color

Color only works once credibility is already secure — "these are like hats, you try them on to see what works for you," as the lecture puts it. Nine named, learnable devices, each demonstrated in a real speech this course assigns:

Ask yourself — is there a moment that uses...
  • Vivid, image-based language — concrete, sensory words instead of abstractions (Churchill's "broad, sunlit uplands")? Related: does a key fact get "more words per square thought" to land, the way Reagan expanded "they took the cliffs" into the full Boys of Pointe du Hoc story?
  • Alliteration — repeated consonant sounds doing real work, not just decoration?
  • Metaphor or analogy — an extended comparison the speech leans on (Cuomo's "tale of two cities" answering Reagan's "shining city on a hill")?
  • Tricolon — a rule-of-three construction ("I came, I saw, I conquered")?
  • Repetition — a phrase repeated deliberately for rhythm (MLK's "let freedom ring")?
  • Quickened pace — sentences shortening and speeding up, or a held pause for effect (FDR's Pearl Harbor address; Emma González's silence)?
  • Rhetorical questions — questions the speaker isn't actually expecting answered?
  • Rounded numbers — a big figure simplified for the ear ("more than 300,000" instead of 304,356)?
  • Pairs — two items offered instead of a long list?

Cook's Chapter 9 adds a layer worth checking separately: tone is a choice independent of content — terse and declarative (Cuomo), discursive, punchy with hard consonants (Malcolm X), soft and soothing (George H.W. Bush), breezy with contractions (Ann Richards), or formal and measured (JFK's inaugural). The same idea can sound completely different depending on which register a speaker picks.

5. Logic & Emotion

The professor's closing framework for the whole term: Know, Feel, Do. Know is logic, feel is emotion, do is credibility/trust (Lens 3) — every part of a speech should be checked against all three.

Logic: facts need "more words per square thought" to land; structure follows problem-solution, often in three parts; openings should earn attention before the roadmap (a stunning unrounded number like 430,222, or a pop-culture reference — not "thank you for having me"); two-sided messaging (state the counterargument briefly, then answer it, sandwiched between positive points) builds credibility by not hiding the other side. But logic has real limits — a Stanford study found people's self-assessments stayed anchored to fake scores even after being told the scores were fabricated, and a Dartmouth-Princeton study found fans of each school blamed the other team for identical dirty play in the same film. Facts alone often can't move an audience whose identity is bound up in a position.

Emotion: stories move people the way statistics can't — three kinds (example, vision, mission). Frame the problem as inconsistent with a value the audience already holds, and the solution as consistent with it (even capuchin monkeys reject a fair reward once they see a neighbor get a better one). Shared history works the same way Lincoln used "four score and seven years ago." Close with urgency (why now), optimism, and deliberate, repeated use of "we."

Ask yourself
  • Know: what is the audience supposed to know after this? Does a key number get "more words per square thought," or does it just fly by?
  • Know: does the opening earn attention before the roadmap, or open with a generic "thank you for having me"?
  • Know: is there a two-sided "sandwich" — the counterargument stated and answered, not ignored?
  • Know's limits: if the audience already disagrees, will more facts actually move them, or is this an identity-invested position that needs something else?
  • Feel: is there a real story (example, vision, or mission), or only statistics?
  • Feel: does it frame the problem and solution against a value the audience already holds?
  • Feel: does it close with urgency, optimism, and a "we"?
  • Run any one section through Know/Feel/Do directly: what does it want me to know, feel, and do?

6. Delivery

Same hierarchy as Week 2, applied to delivery: conviction, connection, then the details. If the first two are real, the details — the things an audience hears and sees — tend to take care of themselves.

What we hear: a conversational baseline punctuated by real variation in volume, rate, and pause (rewatching Oprah's Golden Globes speech with eyes closed reveals it), plus deliberate emphasis — "I didn't say she stole my book," said seven times with a different word stressed each time, produces seven different meanings.

What we see: real eye contact, not "eye dart"; facial expression; physical space and body engagement (more space reads as more powerful); gestures kept in the "power zone" — imagine holding a series of balls from ping-pong to basketball, roughly midsection to shoulders — avoiding both the lectern "death grip" and the "wind in the willows" lean (rewatching Mario Cuomo with the volume off isolates this).

If you're working from a script: mark it up before you deliver it — never "hot off the printer." Slide pages aside rather than flipping them (the "DeSanto slide"), and note in the margins where to speed up, slow down, or hold eye contact.

Ask yourself
  • Close your eyes, or just listen to the audio — is there real variation in volume, rate, and pause, or one flat register?
  • Pick one sentence — how many different meanings could you produce just by moving the emphasis (the "I didn't say she stole my book" test)?
  • Watch with the sound off — is there real eye contact, or "eye dart"?
  • Are gestures staying in the "power zone," or are they notably absent or overdone?
  • Own script: have you marked it up yet — emphasis, pauses, eye-contact notes — or is it about to be delivered blank?

Conviction, connection, then the details. What we hear (tone, volume, rate, pause, emphasis) and what we see (eye contact, body, gestures).
